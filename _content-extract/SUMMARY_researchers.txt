Project description
Project motivation and problem statement
The increasing penetration of converter interfaced renewable energy resources, HVDC transmission systems, battery energy storage systems, and power electronic loads is fundamentally changing the dynamic behaviour of modern power systems. Unlike conventional power systems dominated by synchronous generators, modern converter dominated power systems exhibit strong interactions between AC and DC networks, multiple time scales, and tightly coupled control loops. These characteristics make existing analysis tools insufficient for comprehensive stability assessment. Current software platforms typically focus on only one type of analysis, such as steady state power flow, electromagnetic transient simulation, or small signal analysis, requiring extensive manual model conversion and preventing a unified analysis workflow. This project aims to develop HARMONY, a unified symbolic and numerical framework that integrates power system modelling, optimal power flow, dynamic modelling, and impedance based stability assessment within a single software environment, enabling consistent and automated analysis of converter dominated AC/DC power systems.
How can a unified modelling framework be developed to seamlessly bridge steady-state analysis, dynamic modelling, and frequency-domain stability assessment for converter-dominated hybrid AC/DC power systems?
How can symbolic and numerical computation be integrated to automatically derive component models, assemble network equations, and efficiently perform scalable harmonic stability assessment?
For the second paragraph, write about the challenges. In the third paragraph, write about the approaches you will go for.
Challenges
Developing such a unified framework presents several challenges. First, different analysis methods require fundamentally different mathematical representations, ranging from nonlinear algebraic equations for power flow to state space models and frequency dependent impedance models for stability assessment. Maintaining consistency among these representations while avoiding repeated model development is highly challenging. Second, converter dominated power systems involve heterogeneous components, including modular multilevel converters (MMCs), VSC HVDC transmission systems, renewable energy resources, and battery energy storage systems, each with different control architectures and operating modes that need to be represented within a common modelling framework. Finally, large AC/DC systems introduce significant computational complexity because of large symbolic models, sparse network assembly, and repeated frequency domain calculations, requiring efficient numerical algorithms and a scalable software architecture.
Proposed approach
The proposed research will develop the HARMONY framework based on a modular and hierarchical software architecture. A unified graph based network representation will be established to describe AC/DC systems and component interconnections. Symbolic modelling techniques will automatically generate state space equations, linearized models, and terminal admittance matrices from reusable component descriptions. These symbolic models will then be integrated with numerical solvers for optimal power flow, operating point computation, and impedance based harmonic stability assessment. By combining symbolic computation, sparse numerical algorithms, and automated analysis workflows, HARMONY will provide a unified software platform that substantially reduces modelling effort, maintains consistency across different analysis methods, and enables efficient stability assessment of future converter dominated power systems.
Project keywords
Converter dominated power systems; Hybrid AC/DC networks; Symbolic modelling; Harmonic stability assessment; Optimal power flow; Power system software
Intended deliverables
A modular and open software framework for modelling and analysing converter dominated power systems.
A unified workflow integrating power flow, dynamic modelling, and impedance based stability assessment.
Validation of the framework using representative hybrid AC/DC benchmark systems and practical case studies.

Overview
Inter-oPEn is a doctoral program that integrates multi-sectorial knowledge by gathering electrical engineering and legal researchers.
The doctoral fellows in Inter-oPEn conduct research on the safe and reliable operation of modern power systems dominated by power electronics (PE) and gain additional expertise in academic and industrial secondments.
Openness will be a pivotal factor in their research, tackling various fundamental aspects of modern PE-based electrical systems, such as control, protection, interoperability, governance, and intellectual property challenges.
Project description
The increasing integration of large-scale offshore wind farms into future power-electronics-dominated power systems requires reliable HVDC transmission solutions that can operate without depending on a strong external AC grid. In this context, grid-forming are expected to establish and regulate the AC voltage and frequency, especially during islanded operation, black start, and weak-grid conditions. At the same time, MMC-based multi-terminal HVDC systems are key technologies for transferring offshore wind power to onshore grids, but their performance can be affected by external disturbance (AC-, DC-faults), and internal submodule failures. Therefore, the main problem of this research is how to ensure stable, reliable, and internal submodule fault-tolerant operation of MMC-based HVDC systems for offshore wind integration. The three main research questions are:
How can GFM-based OWFs within HVDC network establish voltage and frequency?
How can an advanced control strategies handle the effect of the unbalanced internal voltage due to internal submodules (SMs) faults (per SM and Cluster SM) of MMC in HVDC network?
How can the proposed control be validated under realistic operating conditions using PHIL?
Future offshore wind farms may operate in weak or isolated AC networks, where conventional grid-following (GFL) control is not sufficient because there may be no strong voltage and frequency reference. So, the GFM control structure and operation must replace the conventional GFL. Second, MMC-based HVDC systems have complex internal dynamics, including arm currents, submodule capacitor voltage balancing, circulating currents, and energy dynamics, which become more critical during internal submodule faults. Internal submodule faults can degrade the converter voltage capability, reduce modulation margins, create imbalance among converter arms, and affect the stability of the overall HVDC network. Then, simulation alone may not fully capture practical converter behavior such as controller implementation effects, measurement delay, and hardware limitations. So, the accuracy of real-time interaction between the physical converter and the simulated power system is main challenge to perform PHIL.
This research will develop and assess control strategies for grid-forming offshore wind farms integrated with MMC-based HVDC systems. The first approach is to design grid-forming control for wind turbine converters so that the offshore wind farm can regulate offshore voltage and frequency and support stable operation during islanded, weak-grid, and fault conditions. The second approach is to study the impact of internal MMC faults and develop fault-tolerant control strategies to maintain converter operation and reduce performance degradation after submodule failures. The third approach is to validate the proposed control system using Power Hardware-in-the-Loop experiments, where real converter hardware or controller hardware is interfaced with a real-time simulated HVDC network.
Intended deliverables
GFM control framework for offshore wind farms within MMC-HVDC networks
Development of control strategies enabling OWFs to establish and regulate offshore voltage and frequency during islanded, weak-grid, black-start, and fault conditions.
Internal submodule fault-tolerant control for MMC-based MT-HVDC systems
Analysis and mitigation of MMC performance degradation caused by individual and clustered submodule faults, including voltage imbalance, reduced modulation margin, and arm energy deviation.
Real-time simulation and PHIL validation platform
Implementation and validation of the proposed control strategies under realistic operating conditions, considering controller delay, hardware limitations, and physical interaction with the simulated HVDC network.

PROSECCO employs Model-Based Systems Engineering (MBSE) to ensure a consistent, vendor-neutral approach from the design stage. More specifically, we focus on exploring novel control techniques that help mitigate faults and, consequently, enhance system reliability. In this project, we leverage robust control, model predictive control, and machine learning methods to design controllers that can adapt to fault conditions and ensure the stability of both individual converters and the overall network.

PhD/ Postdoc project name: Coordinated Optimal Control of Hybrid Power Systems
Staring year: 2022

Project description
The large-scale integration of offshore wind, enabled by voltage source converter (VSC)-based high-voltage direct current (HVDC) technology, is giving rise to hybrid AC–multi-terminal HVDC (MTDC) power systems that combine AC-grid stability with DC-network controllability. The development of hybrid AC-MTDC systems has become a critical area of research due to the increasing integration of renewable energy sources, particularly offshore wind, and the need for flexible, stable, and optimized power delivery. This project develops an optimal power flow (OPF)-integrated coordinated droop control framework that unifies system-level optimization with decentralized converter control while explicitly accounting for wind-generation uncertainty. It addresses:
Research question 1: How to develop an OPF model for a hybrid AC–MTDC system?
Research question 2: How can the local control strategies of VSC converter stations be integrated into the OPF model of hybrid AC–MTDC systems?
Research question 3: How to model the uncertainties arising from offshore wind generation in the control strategy?
Three challenges arise. Formulating an OPF for hybrid AC–MTDC networks requires capturing the nonconvex, tightly coupled AC–DC feasible set and converter limits that standard AC formulations omit. Integrating local droop control must reconcile economic optimality with decentralized autonomy while jointly regulating DC voltage and AC frequency. Finally, offshore wind forecast errors are heteroscedastic and non-stationary, so embedding this uncertainty into a large-scale OPF without sacrificing computational efficiency or real-time feasibility remains difficult.
The work proceeds in three stages. First, an OPF model is developed for hybrid AC–MTDC systems, incorporating VSC/modular multilevel converter (MMC) models and AC/DC network constraints. Second, droop control is embedded through a hierarchical two-stage formulation in which DC-voltage stability precedes generation-cost minimization, with frequency droop loops in the MMCs enabling joint DC-voltage and AC-frequency regulation. Third, wind variability is handled via two complementary extensions: a forecast-integrated anticipatory controller validated by hardware-in-the-loop (HIL)/real-time digital simulator (RTDS) testing, and a chance-constrained stochastic OPF using polynomial chaos expansion (PCE) for closed-form adaptive droop extraction. Validation uses multi-terminal test systems with quantitative benchmarking against baseline forecasts and multiple control modes.
Project keywords
Hybrid AC–MTDC systems; Optimal power flow (OPF); Coordinated droop control; Voltage–frequency regulation; Offshore wind uncertainty; Voltage source converters (VSC)
Intended deliverables
An OPF model for hybrid AC–MTDC systems with offshore wind integration.
A coordinated voltage–frequency droop control strategy embedded in the OPF framework.
Two uncertainty-aware extensions: a forecast-integrated anticipatory controller and a PCE-based chance-constrained stochastic OPF.
Real-time HIL/RTDS validation on multi-terminal test systems, benchmarked against a persistence-baseline forecast and multiple control modes.
A doctoral dissertation and peer-reviewed conference/journal publications.

Aleksandra Lekić, Peter Palensky, Pedro P. Vergara

Project description
The rapid integration of inverter-based resources and converter interface generation (CIG) links into transmission grids is producing converter-dominated systems whose stability behaviour cannot be adequately captured by classical phasor-domain tools, yet full EMT simulation remains computationally costly at network scale. Dynamic phasor (DP) modelling offers a principled middle ground, retaining selected harmonic dynamics through time-varying Fourier coefficients without switching-level resolution. Still, its systematic application to hybrid AC/MTDC systems using a state-space formulation is underdeveloped, and no open-source framework currently unifies DP-based simulation with state-space and harmonic stability assessment.
Research questions:
Can a unified DP-based framework simultaneously support harmonic state-space analysis and impedance-based stability assessment in hybrid AC/DC systems?
What does the DP transformation uniquely reveal and what does it conceal in the frequency-domain stability assessment of converter-dominated MTDC systems compared to RMS and EMT approaches?
How do higher-order harmonic interactions and DC-side dynamics affect small-signal stability boundaries in converter-dense MTDC networks, and can participation factor analysis identify the dominant harmonic contributors?
How can dynamic phasor models of hybrid AC/DC systems be used to systematically identify and compensate dynamics that are invisible to static phasor-based controller design, and what is the resulting improvement in stability margins?
The core difficulty is that the CIG multi-timescale dynamics (arm currents, capacitor balancing, outer control loops) generate harmonic couplings between the AC and DC sides that scalar phasor models miss entirely, hence, deriving algebraically consistent state-space and admittance representations for a full hybrid network without manual per-component derivation requires a systematic symbolic pipeline.
This research adopts dynamic phasor modelling as the core analytical framework, positioning it as the principled middle ground between RMS and full EMT approaches for hybrid AC/DC systems. As known, DPs represent a nearly periodic signal as a sum of time-varying Fourier coefficients rotating at harmonic frequencies , capturing power-electronic dynamics that static phasors discard while avoiding the switching-level resolution that EMT captures. The DQsym formulation transforms these coefficients into slowly varying DQ-frame state variables, enabling a unified state-space representation that simultaneously supports eigenvalue-based small-signal stability analysis and impedance-based harmonic assessment, directly addressing the research gaps. The framework is validated progressively, with EMT simulations in MATLAB/Simulink serving as reference throughout.
Project keywords
Dynamic phasors, Hybrid AC/DC power systems, Harmonic stability analysis, Converter-dominated power systems, Small-signal stability.
Intended deliverables
Full Dynamic Phasor Framework. A comprehensive dynamic phasor (DP)-based modeling framework supporting simulation, control design, and stability analysis for hybrid AC/DC power systems.
Open-source simulation library: A fully validated library implementing the DQsym formulation across a comprehensive component set, with documented mathematical derivations, unit-tested algorithms, and reproducible benchmark results against EMT references across RLC, point-to-point HVDC, and IEEE 9-bus hybrid AC/DC test cases.
Systematic state-space formulation pipeline: A symbolic-to-numerical workflow for automatically deriving consistent ABCD state-space representations of arbitrary hybrid AC/DC network topologies via Modified Nodal Analysis (MNA), supporting both component-level and system-level linearisation around operating points obtained from power flow solutions.
Integrated stability assessment procedure: A complete analysis workflow encompassing AC/MTDC optimal power flow for operating point initialization, linearisation and eigenvalue analysis with participation factor decomposition for mode identification; impedance-based harmonic stability assessment, and Bode and Nyquist plots of the feedback transfer function enabling stability assessment of converter-dominated grids.

Project description
Modern PE-based converter control typically operates on the millisecond time scale and is therefore incapable of responding quickly to unforeseen system changes and grid disruptions. This limited speed and adaptability create a risk of large voltage deviations, power overshoots, and even blackouts in converter-dominated HVDC grids. The ground-breaking innovativeness of this project stems from the development of a smart control methodology that is computationally efficient and reduces control execution time to the microsecond range, while simultaneously improving the controller’s adaptive response to grid uncertainties and large disturbances by introducing a data-driven component into Model Predictive Control. This methodology aims to guarantee stable grid operation even under unforeseen disturbances, accelerate fault elimination by minimizing voltage deviations and power overshoots, and ensure fast grid protection operation, thereby significantly increasing the reliability of PE-based electrical grids.
Research question 1: How can we develop a robust and scalable control strategy for power electronic converters in multi-terminal, multi-vendor HVDC systems to ensure stable DC voltage and reliable power transfer under normal and disturbed operating conditions?
Research question 2: How can we design and validate interoperability-oriented control and protection frameworks that enable standardized coordination and data exchange between converters and TSOs, while addressing multi-vendor compatibility and disturbance resilience in large-scale PE-dominated grids?
The main challenges include the high dimensionality and nonlinearity of converter-dominated HVDC grids, the presence of multiple interacting control layers, and the need to coordinate ultra-fast local converter actions with system-level reliability requirements. Additional difficulties arise from multi-vendor interoperability, the need for standardized data exchange between converters and TSOs, and the requirement to design disturbance-resilient control and protection mechanisms (for faults, line outages, overloads, and other severe events) that prevent cascading failures and support secure operation of large-scale PE-based grids.
The project will address these challenges by developing a rigorous data-driven Model Predictive Control framework tailored to PE-based HVDC grids. The smart controller will be implemented in real-time environments (RSCAD) as an open-access control unit prototype for PE converters, together with a comprehensive HVDC grid model library.

Development of a comprehensive smart controller for PE-based power systems enabling extremely fast grid start-up, robust response to catastrophic disturbances, and blackout prevention with fast black start capability.
Implementation of an open-access control unit prototype for PE converters and a real-time simulation library for HVDC-based electrical grids (RTDS/RTDS-like), supported by validated models that mitigate failures caused by slow control dynamics.

Type: PhD [  ]  or  Postdoc  [X]
PhD/Postdoc researcher name: Hao Xu
PhD/ Postdoc project name: MITIGATE-HARM
Staring year: 2025
Funder organization/Project: Clean Energy Transition Partnership (CETP)
Project description
The rapid growth of renewable generation and power-electronic (PE) converters is reshaping power-system dynamics. Operating at high frequencies with complex switching, PE converters can act as a negative resistance across a wide frequency band and, when interconnected in hybrid AC/DC grids, may drive the system into oscillations, resonances, and instabilities that threaten secure operation. This project develops fast, non-vendor-specific methods to identify such undesired behaviour and to mitigate it, implemented in the open-source Harmony toolbox. It addresses the following research questions:
Research question 1: How can converter-driven instabilities and harmonic resonances in large-scale hybrid AC/DC (multi-terminal, multi-vendor HVDC) power systems be identified accurately and efficiently using impedance-based stability assessment?
Research question 2: Which mitigation measures — for example controller-loop tuning, wideband passivation, or parameter/topology changes — most effectively remove these instabilities while preserving stable, reliable operation, and how can they be automated within the toolbox?
The main challenges are the wide frequency range over which PE converters interact with the grid, which requires a fully dynamic system model; the need to work with black-box, IP-protected vendor converter models; the fragmentation of existing tools across platforms (MATLAB, PSCAD, RSCAD) and the resulting long, multi-tool simulation times; and the lack of unified benchmarks and field-validated datasets for resonance and instability analysis. Achieving accuracy comparable to commercial tools together with dramatically faster execution, while keeping the methods general enough to apply to real TSO networks, is a central difficulty.
To address these challenges, the project builds a mathematical framework for instability and resonance identification and extends it with mitigation and wideband-passivity strategies, implemented in C++ inside the Harmony toolbox for execution in seconds on general-purpose CPUs. Methods are first formulated and tested against the commercial simulator PSCAD (with the Z-tool) and cross-validated with eigenvalue-based analysis in DPsim, then applied to real-life large-scale test cases provided by TenneT. Where useful, machine-learning and adaptive-control elements support parameter estimation and control tuning. Results are benchmarked against defined KPIs and released open-source, together with representative test cases contributed to CRESYM's Collaborative Dynamic Library.
Project keywords
HVDC; power-electronics-based power systems; converter-driven stability; resonance stability; impedance-based stability assessment; harmonic mitigation.
Intended deliverables
A mathematical framework and open-source implementation (C++) for identifying instabilities and resonances in the Harmony toolbox.
Mitigation and wideband-passivity methods integrated into the Harmony toolbox to restore stable operation.
Verification against PSCAD/Z-tool and cross-validation with DPsim on IEEE 9-bus and Nordic benchmark systems.
Application and validation on real-life TenneT test cases, with evaluation of the technical KPIs.
Peer-reviewed journal and conference publications, and public test cases contributed to CRESYM's Collaborative Dynamic Library.

Project Description
Power electronic (PE) devices play a crucial role in enabling the integration of renewable energy sources into modern power systems. However, to achieve interoperability of PE devices, barriers are present in technology but also in intellectual property and regulation. Inter-oPEn addresses these challenges through a unique doctoral training program for 10 researchers, combining expertise from both electrical engineering and legal disciplines. To achieve the common goal of the interoperable PE-dominated power system, openness will be a pivotal factor across the different doctoral projects, tackling fundamental aspects of modern PE-based electrical systems such as control, protection, interoperability, governance, and intellectual property challenges. Project description
The global transition toward renewable energy and the increasing demand for flexible power transmission have accelerated the deployment of HVDC and hybrid AC/DC systems. Multi-terminal HVDC networks are essential for integrating large-scale renewable energy resources and interconnecting asynchronous grids; however, their operation introduces significant challenges due to complex converter dynamics, evolving system topologies, and the interaction between control and protection systems. Advanced control strategies are required to maintain system stability, coordinate converter operation, and support dynamic grid conditions, while advanced protection schemes are needed to achieve fast, selective, and reliable fault detection and isolation. This research aims to develop advanced control and protection frameworks for future HVDC and hybrid AC/DC systems.
Research question 1: How can advanced DC protection algorithms be developed to achieve fast, selective, and reliable fault detection and isolation in multi-terminal HVDC systems?
Research question 2: How can control and protection coordination strategies enable adaptive performance under changing converter control modes, system topology variations, and dynamic operating conditions?
Research question 3: How can controller hardware-in-the-loop (cHIL) platforms be utilized to validate the performance and resilience of advanced control and protection schemes under realistic HVDC and hybrid AC/DC system scenarios?
The main challenges include the complex interaction between control and protection systems, the fast dynamics of DC faults, and the difficulty of achieving accurate fault detection under varying operating conditions. In addition, the increasing integration of renewable energy sources and power electronic converters introduces significant variability and dynamic behavior in DC systems, requiring advanced control strategies to maintain voltage stability, power flow regulation, converter coordination, and system resilience during normal and disturbed operating conditions. Furthermore, validating the proposed control and protection methods in realistic environments requires advanced simulation and real-time testing platforms.
The project will address these challenges by developing novel control and DC protection algorithms, supported by detailed system modeling, simulation studies, and control hardware-in-the-loop (CHIL) validation. The proposed approaches will focus on improving converter coordination, adaptive system operation, and fast fault detection and isolation. The developed methods will be evaluated under different operating scenarios to assess their effectiveness and enhance the reliability, stability, and performance of future multi-terminal DC power systems.

Overview
MITIGATE-HARM is a European research and innovation project that makes future power systems more resilient and reliable by identifying, assessing, and mitigating converter-driven instabilities and harmonic resonances in large-scale hybrid AC/DC power systems, including multi-terminal and multi-vendor HVDC grids. As grids integrate more renewable energy and power-electronic devices, new fast dynamics and stability challenges emerge that existing, vendor-specific tools cannot assess quickly enough. MITIGATE-HARM develops fast, non-vendor-specific stability-assessment tools built on two open-source platforms — Harmony (impedance-based analysis) and DPsim (eigenvalue-based analysis) — that support transmission system operators (TSOs) already at the early planning stage. By enabling rapid simulation and systematic mitigation strategies, the project enhances grid resilience, interoperability, and planning efficiency, facilitating the safe integration of up to 100% renewable energy.
Project description
The digitalisation of power systems and the large-scale integration of renewable energy sources have sharply increased the penetration of power-electronic (PE) equipment. Operating at high frequencies with complex switching dynamics, PE converters introduce fast new dynamics and expand the classical power-system stability classification to include converter-driven and resonance stability. Because a converter can behave as a negative resistance over a wide frequency range, its interaction with the rest of the grid can push the system into unstable operation, producing oscillations and resonances that, in severe cases, may lead to blackouts. These risks grow with the move toward hybrid AC/DC grids and multi-terminal, multi-vendor HVDC systems, and are aggravated by two roadblocks: the lack of standardisation for PE control and data exchange with TSO control rooms, and the vendor- and case-specific nature of current projects, which limits interoperability between equipment and future extensions.
MITIGATE-HARM addresses these challenges by developing a mathematical framework able to simulate all components of a hybrid power system for converter-driven stability assessment within a single toolbox, complemented by cross-validation across two approaches. On the impedance-based side, the project extends the open-source Harmony toolbox with new functionality to identify instabilities and resonances and to propose mitigation measures and wideband passivity, all implemented in C++. On the eigenvalue side, it extends the open-source DPsim solver to model, simulate, and perform eigenvalue analysis of hybrid AC/DC grids, and integrates DPsim as a solver within OPAL-RT's real-time simulation platform. The developments are verified and cross-validated on benchmark systems (an IEEE 9-bus system with HVDC links and a Nordic test system with PE converters) against commercial and open-source tools (PSCAD, Z-tool, OPAL-RT), and then applied to real-life, large-scale test cases provided by the TSO TenneT.
Through this work, Harmony advances from TRL 3 to TRL 5 and the new DPsim capabilities from TRL 2–3 to TRL 5. Success is tracked by concrete KPIs: below 1% inaccuracy on calculated instabilities and resonances relative to commercial tools, at least an order-of-magnitude reduction in simulation time (from minutes to seconds), and more than 30% reduction of identified instabilities through the proposed mitigation and wideband-passivity measures. The project also establishes open benchmark suites and public datasets for dynamic-model validation via EMT–HIL measurements, published through CRESYM's Collaborative Dynamic Library. Together, these results give TSOs proactive tools to detect, prevent, and diagnose instabilities and resonances, strengthening the resilience, interoperability, and planning of future hybrid AC/DC grids and supporting the safe integration of up to 100% renewable energy.
Intended deliverables
Advanced Harmony toolbox: an extended open-source C++ framework adding instability and resonance identification, mitigation measures, and wideband passivity, with user-friendly interfaces and field configurability.
Enhanced DPsim toolbox: modelling, simulation, and eigenvalue analysis of hybrid AC/DC grids, plus integration of the DPsim solver into OPAL-RT's real-time simulation platform, released open-source with documentation and examples.
Open benchmark suites and public datasets: standardised testbeds for dynamic-model validation focused on resonance paths and instability identification, validated with EMT–HIL measurements and published via CRESYM's Collaborative Dynamic Library (CoLib).
Verification, cross-validation, and application on real-life TenneT test cases, with evaluation of the technical KPIs and a preliminary pan-European scalability analysis.
Scientific dissemination: at least three gold open-access journal papers and three conference papers, plus workshops, webinars, tutorials, and special sessions.
Project communication and legacy: project website, LinkedIn, and Zenodo channels; a Data Management Plan; training modules; and long-term maintenance of the toolboxes as Linux Foundation Energy projects.
Project partners
The MITIGATE-HARM consortium brings together six partners from four European countries. It is coordinated by Delft University of Technology (TU Delft, Netherlands) and includes three academic institutions active in power-electronics-dominated power systems — TU Delft, the Aristotle University of Thessaloniki (AUTH, Greece), and RWTH Aachen University (Germany) — together with the transmission system operator TenneT TSO GmbH (Germany), the real-time hardware-in-the-loop simulator manufacturer OPAL-RT Germany GmbH (Germany), and CRESYM (Collaborative Research for Energy System Modelling, Belgium), a non-profit association fostering open-source energy-system research between academia and industry. This mix of academia, a TSO, an industrial simulator manufacturer, and an open-source organisation, spanning northern and south-eastern Europe, combines complementary expertise in stability analysis, modelling and simulation, real-time testing, and dissemination, and builds on the partners' long track record of joint projects such as Harmony, InterOPERA, HVDC-WISE, Inter-oPEn, and EASY-RES.
